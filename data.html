    Where the data comes from, and where it goes

    Before you see a team's points on this site, the numbers go through six steps.

    1. 1

      Teams play matches

      All over the world, teams play matches in tournaments. Some on stage in front of a crowd, some online from home.

      The grown-up nameCounter-Strike 2 events

    2. 2

      Someone writes them down

      Websites write down every match: who played, who won, and how much prize money each team took home.

      The grown-up nameHLTV for Valve, Liquipedia for OpenVRS

    3. 3

      We save a copy

      We save every list exactly as it was published. We never change this copy, so we can always check where a number comes from.

      The grown-up nameBronze · Google Cloud Storage

    4. 4

      We put it in tables and check it

      We write the data in tables, with one row per team or per match, and check that nothing is missing or wrong. If a check fails, everything stops.

      The grown-up nameSilver · BigQuery tables and quality checks

    5. 5

      We do the maths

      We compute each team's points with the same formulas as Valve. Then the computer plays the next events thousands of times to count who gets a place at the Major.

      The grown-up nameGold · the model in Python and the simulation

    6. 6

      A program builds the website

      Twice a day, a program starts on its own, looks for new matches and builds every page again, in English and in French.

      The grown-up nameCloud Run job at 06:00 and 18:00 UTC · GitHub Pages

    Old matches count less

    A match from last week counts fully. A match from five months ago counts only a little. A match older than six months does not count at all.

    How a team gets its points

    The points are computed in two parts. Part 1 looks at the last six months and gives each team a starting number. Part 2 goes through the matches one by one, in the order they were played, and adds or removes points.

    Part 1: four scores, then the average

    Each team gets four scores. Each score is a number between 0 and 1. A team gets 1 when it does as well as the team ranked 5th, or better. Then we take the average of the four scores: each one counts for a quarter.

    s=O+C+N+L4
    O=c(min(1,MM5))
    M=∑10 best resultsprize×a
    C=c(110∑10 best winsOopp×a×c(P1,000,000))
    N=110∑10 best winsmin(1,DoppD5)×a×c(P1,000,000)
    D=∑teams beatena
    L=min(1,WW5)
    W=110∑10 best LAN winsa
    c(x)=11+|log10x|
    O
    the prize money the team won, in its 10 best results.
    C
    the prize money of the teams it beat.
    N
    how many different teams it beat, and how good those teams are.
    L
    its wins at LAN events, where both teams play in the same room and not over the internet.
    a
    the age weight of a result, between 0 and 1 (the chart above).
    P
    the prize pool of the event, up to 1,000,000 dollars. Wins at events with more prize money count more.
    c
    turns a ratio into a score: 1 stays 1, 0.1 becomes 0.5, 0.01 becomes 0.33. A team with 10 times less money does not get a score 10 times smaller.
    M₅, D₅, W₅
    the same number for the team ranked 5th.

    The average is turned into a starting number between 400 and 2,000 points. The team with the lowest average gets 400, the team with the highest gets 2,000.

    start=400+1,600×s−sminsmax−smin

    Part 2: one match at a time

    Before each match, the formula computes E, the chance it gives the team to win. After the match, S is 1 if the team won and 0 if it lost. The team gains points when it does better than expected and loses points when it does worse.

    E=11+10−g(R−Ropp)/400
    R′≈R+k×w×(S−E)
    R, Ropp
    the team's points before the match, and the opponent's points.
    w
    the age weight of the match.
    k
    the size of a step, about 30. A recent win against a team with the same points gives 15 points.

    The exact formula is Glicko with a fixed deviation of 75 (Glickman, 1999), so g is 0.97 and k moves a little, between 30 and 32.

    What decides the points in practice

    In the formula, the four scores count the same. But among the 30 best teams, they do not change by the same amount from one team to another. A score that is almost the same for every team cannot separate them.

    How much each score changes among the 30 best teams (standard deviation × 100)

    On the 2 Nov 2026 standings, the score that changes the most is O · prize money won.

    Part 1 gives the 30 best teams starting numbers that are 177 points apart (standard deviation). Part 2 then moves each of them by 36 points on average, 121 at most. So part 1 decides most of the ranking.

    What we do at each layer

    Between the published files and this site, the data goes through three layers, called bronze, silver and gold. Each layer does one job. A layer only starts when the one before it has finished without errors.

    Bronze: keep the files as they were published

    • We save each file exactly as it was published, byte for byte, in Google Cloud Storage.
    • We never change a saved file. A new version is saved as a new file.
    • For each file we compute a checksum: a number calculated from all the bytes of the file. If one byte changes, the number changes, so we can show the file is the original.
    • We write the list of files, the manifest, last. If it is missing, the next layer does not start.

    Silver: turn the files into checked tables

    • We read every file and write its data in BigQuery tables, with one row per team, per match or per prize.
    • Each column has a fixed type: a date must be a date, a number must be a number. No row may appear twice.
    • Each match appears on the page of both teams. We check that both pages give the same result.
    • If one check fails, nothing is written and the run stops.
    • On the 2 Nov 2026 standings: 376 teams and 11,146 match rows.

    Gold: compute the points and the chances

    • We compute every team's points with the formulas above, rewritten in Python from Valve's model.
    • We compare our numbers with the points Valve published: they match for 376 teams out of 376.
    • The computer plays the next events 5,000 times and counts how often each team gets a place at the Major.
    • The results go into the tables that this site reads.
